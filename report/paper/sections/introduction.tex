\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Reasoning models write out their working before they answer~\cite{wei2022}, and
Qwen3 offers a thinking and a non-thinking mode in one model~\cite{qwen3}. Readers may
take the working as an explanation of how the answer was reached. But an explanation
can be plausible without being faithful to the process behind the
prediction~\cite{jacovi2020}, and training a model to answer correctly does not
require its working to be an honest account.

Most faithfulness tests intervene: they corrupt or truncate the reasoning and watch
the answer~\cite{lanham2023}, or add a hint to the prompt and check whether the
reasoning mentions it~\cite{turpin2023,chen2025}. We first ask a narrower question of
the model's own, unedited output: is the final answer \emph{consistent} with the
written arithmetic? This can be checked formally and without gold labels. Consistency
is weaker than faithfulness~\cite{parcalabescu2024}, so we pair it with two
interventions. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item an exact-arithmetic consistency checker for natural-language working, and a Z3
  logic checker~\cite{demoura2008} validated on FOLIO annotations~\cite{han2024folio};
\item a consistency result for Qwen3-1.7B on 50 GSM8K problems~\cite{cobbe2021}: no
  contradictions in 83 comparable responses, with accuracy 92\% with thinking and
  80\% without;
\item a copy-vs-use control on Qwen3-0.6B, indicating that, at the first answer digit
  of constructed prompts, sensitivity to planted working mostly reflects copying its
  last number rather than computing with it;
\item a misleading-hint test on Qwen3-1.7B (\textbf{TBD}), with its hardware
  confound stated.
\end{itemize}
